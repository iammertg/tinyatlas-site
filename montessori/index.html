Inspired by Montessori

Tiny Atlas is not a Montessori programme, and no one has certified it. It is a small play app that borrows a handful of ideas from Montessori classrooms and tries to keep them on a screen. Here is what that means, where you can see it in the worlds, and where a screen falls short.

The child leads

In a Montessori room the child chooses the work and the adult stays in the background. Tiny Atlas opens on twelve round windows and nothing else. Your child picks one, goes in, and comes back out with the little house button whenever they like. There are no levels to unlock, no path to follow and no voice telling them what to do next. Everything meant for grown-ups, such as buying, settings and links, sits behind a gate that a two-year-old will not open by accident.

Real life first

Montessori puts the real before the make-believe. Most of the worlds are places a child already knows: a kitchen with a pot of soup, a garden to plant and water, a farm, a pond, a busy street, a music room. The animals do not talk, and the sounds are gentle and close to the real ones. Stirring a pot, putting a seed in the soil, loading an egg onto the farm cart: these are everyday jobs a small child sees around them, and the worlds are built on them.

No stars, no stickers

There are no scores, stars, coins or badges, and nobody says “well done”. The reward is what the world does: the soup changes colour, the seed grows into a flower, the snowman goes to stand in the row. What a child makes stays where they left it, so they can come back to it tomorrow.

One thing at a time, and nothing is wrong

Montessori materials isolate one idea and let a child see their own mistakes without an adult correcting them. In Tiny Atlas each thing does one clear thing when it is touched, and the same touch gives the same answer every time, so a child can repeat it as often as they like. There is no red cross, no “try again” and no “finished” screen. Nearly everything can be undone, and whatever a child tries is allowed.

A calm, ordered room

A Montessori classroom is a prepared environment: quiet, tidy, with a place for everything. The windows stay in the same place on the shelf every day. The colours are soft, there is no background music, and most things move only when a child touches them. There are no ads, no pop-ups and no timers.

Showing, not telling

A Montessori teacher presents a material by doing it, slowly and with few words. Tiny Atlas has no words to read and no spoken instructions; the things in each world invite a touch on their own. Young children learn a great deal by watching you, which is why the suggestions below start with sitting together.

Freedom, within limits

Montessori gives children real freedom inside a few clear limits. In a world, your child can touch anything, in any order, as many times as they like, and several fingers at once are welcome. The limits are the ones a grown-up sets: how long to play, and the gate in front of buying and settings. A locked window never shows your child a price; it only gives a little shake.

Where we differ

A screen is not a Montessori material. In Montessori, learning happens through the hands, with real objects, real water and real flour, and no app can take the place of that. Tiny Atlas works best as a short, calm moment: in a waiting room, before dinner, on a slow afternoon. It does not replace the kitchen or the garden; at most it opens a door to them. Some worlds also step into fantasy: outer space has a little UFO, and the dinosaur valley has a friendly T-rex. We kept them because children love them, knowing that a strict Montessori eye would raise an eyebrow.

A few suggestions for grown-ups

  • Sit with your child the first time. Touch something yourself, slowly, and let them watch. Say little.
  • Keep sessions short, and stop while it is still fun.
  • Carry it into real life: after the kitchen, let them stir a real pot; after the garden, water a real plant.
  • The discovery journal in the grown-ups’ area tells you what your child found, and suggests one thing to ask them about.
  • If your child tends to slip out of the app, Guided Access keeps them in it; the support page shows how.
